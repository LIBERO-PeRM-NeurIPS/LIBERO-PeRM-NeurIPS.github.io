Abstract

Robots deployed in homes will serve users whose expectations go beyond task success: two people may request the same task yet want it executed in very different ways. We present LIBERO-PeRM, a benchmark for personalized robotic manipulation built on LIBERO that treats user preference as a first-class variable of task execution. Preferences are organized in a three-tier hierarchy — motion-level style (speed, acceleration, carrying height, path shape, obstacle clearance, rotation), skill-level habits (how to grasp, how to release), and user-profile-level plans (which object to use, in what order) — and instantiated across 300+ preference-annotated mission configurations with an automated, preference-aware demonstration generation pipeline.

Five task suites organize the benchmark around five research questions: can frozen VLAs follow preferences zero-shot; can preferences be learned; at what data cost; do learned preferences compose; and what happens under conflict. Beyond binary success, every rollout is scored with a tiered preference-satisfaction metric, calibrated between preferred and preference-agnostic (“naive”) demonstrations.

Zero-shot evaluation of six state-of-the-art VLAs reveals a consistent personalization gap: appending a single preference clause to the instruction drops success rates by 31–56 points across capable models, while preference-aware fine-tuning recovers most of the gap — establishing LIBERO-PeRM as both a diagnosis of, and a training ground for, personalized manipulation.

Why Personalized Manipulation?

Benchmarks for language-conditioned manipulation ask “did the robot complete the task?” — a binary notion of success that ignores how the task was done. Real users care about the how: keep drinks away from the laptop, carry the knife low, slow down near the glassware, use the blue mug because it is mine.

We recognize preference as the cornerstone of personalization. A preference is an additional language clause attached to a task instruction that constrains execution without changing the goal. LIBERO-PeRM asks whether current VLAs can parse such clauses, ground them in behavior, learn them from demonstrations, compose them — and stay loyal to the task when a preference fights it.

A Three-Tier Preference System

Preferences act at three levels of the manipulation stack, from continuous motion style to discrete task plans.

Motion

low-level · continuous

Style constraints on the end-effector trajectory, checked per-step from physical state.

  • “Move slower” speed
  • “Accelerate gently” acceleration
  • “Carry it higher above the table” height
  • “Take the direct path” straightness
  • “Keep away from the wine bottle” avoidance
  • “Don't tilt it while moving” rotation

Skill

mid-level · discrete choice

Habits in how a primitive skill is performed — same skill, different manner.

  • “Pick it up from the side” grasp style
  • “Grab it by the rim / edge” grasp point
  • “Release it just above the basket” release height

User Profile

high-level · task plan

Preferences that reshape the task plan itself: object choice and subtask order.

  • “Prefer the blue objects” object choice
  • “Do the bowl first” ordering
  • “Use my mug, not the glass” ownership

The Benchmark: Five Task Suites

LIBERO-PeRM extends LIBERO scenes with preference-conditioned layouts and language. An automated pipeline — LLM-assisted layout generation, a library of 30+ heuristic manipulation skills, and collision-free motion planning — renders preference-consistent demonstrations for every mission, alongside preference-agnostic “naive” twins used for metric calibration. Each demonstration ships with gen-time metadata: subtask boundaries, applied preference parameters, and per-preference raw measurements.

Five Research Questions

A capability story in five acts: from raw ability, through cost, to compositional structure and robustness.

Can it?→ Can it learn?→ At what cost?→ Does it compose?→ Does it survive conflict?
RQ1

Zero-shot personalization

Can a frozen VLA follow a preference just from language — single and multi, canonical and paraphrased? If yes, training is redundant; if no, that is the benchmark's core negative result.

RQ2

Learning ability

Given ample in-distribution preference demonstrations, does LoRA fine-tuning reach a preference-following ceiling? The gap to RQ1 is the inference-time personalization deficit.

RQ3

Learning efficiency

Sweeping (K layouts, N demos) jointly, where does the learning curve saturate? Turns “can it learn” into “is it affordable to teach”.

RQ4

Preference composition

Train on A and B separately — can the model execute A+B? Train on A+B jointly — can it still do B alone? Probes whether preference representations are disentangled.

RQ5

Preference conflict

Opposing preferences, and preferences that physically contradict the task. Under a task-first stance, we measure whether the model stays loyal to the task, yields to the preference, or collapses.

Beyond Success Rate: Tiered Preference Satisfaction

Every rollout receives a task success rate (BDDL goal predicates) and a preference-satisfaction score per tier. Each preference defines a raw physical measurement — peak acceleration, carrying height, minimum clearance to the avoided object, subtask-plan compliance — which is dual-anchor calibrated: preference-agnostic naive demonstrations anchor 0, preferred demonstrations anchor 1. Calibrated scores map to four satisfaction buckets, making numbers comparable across preference types and missions.

Under conflict, the benchmark takes a task-first stance: the correct behavior is to complete the task and ignore the contradicting preference. Dedicated diagnostics — Override Resistance, Priority Index, and Spurious Preference Application Rate — characterize where a model sits between task loyalty and preference obedience.

Key Findings

1

The personalization gap is real. Every capable VLA loses 31–56 points of success the moment a single preference clause is appended — current models parse the task but not the how.

2

Preferences are learnable. LoRA fine-tuning on preference-consistent demonstrations lifts π0.5-base to 85% preference-conditioned success on the same suite — the ceiling exists.

3

How you mix data matters. Sequentially fine-tuning an already-LIBERO-tuned checkpoint on preference data underperforms (74.4% vs. 85%); joint mixing of generic and preference demonstrations is the stronger recipe.

4

A layout-shift stress test for free. Models trained per-suite on stock LIBERO collapse under LIBERO-PeRM's modest scene edits (96.7% → 17.9% on matched missions), while multi-suite-trained models keep 70–91% — the benchmark doubles as a generalization probe.
